What a Paver Patio Costs

Two quotes for the same patio, one noticeably cheaper. Nine times out of ten the difference is not the pavers. It is how much of the ground came out before they went down.

Updated August 2026

Where the difference between two quotes lives

A finished patio is a surface. A patio job is mostly a hole.

By the time a paver field is laid, the largest share of the labor and a large share of the material has already been buried.

Excavated soil hauled away, base material brought in, compacted in layers, and a bedding course screeded flat. None of that is visible when the job is done, and none of it can be added later.

Which is why the honest way to compare two patio quotes is to compare what each one says about the part you will never see.

If one quote specifies an excavation depth and a base thickness and the other says "install pavers", you are not looking at the same job at two prices. You are looking at two different jobs.

The layers, and why each one is on the bill

You do not need to know how to build this. You do need to know that each of these is a line of cost somebody either included or did not.

  1. Excavation. Ground comes out to a depth that depends on the soil and on whether anything will drive on the finished surface. Every inch of extra depth is more soil to move.
  2. Disposal. That soil goes somewhere. Spread on site is free. Loaded and hauled is not, and on a small suburban lot there is rarely anywhere to spread it.
  3. Base material. Crushed stone brought in by the ton, placed and compacted in layers rather than dumped. This is usually the largest single material line.
  4. Bedding. A thin, precisely screeded layer the units sit in.
  5. The surface. The part everyone is choosing between, and often not the biggest number.
  6. The edge. Restraint around the perimeter so the outer courses cannot spread.
  7. Joint material. Sand swept in, sometimes a polymeric type that resists washing out.

Details of how that work is actually carried out are on the hardscape page. What matters here is that a quote which never mentions layers two through four has priced a thinner job.

The surfaces, ranked

Least expensive installed to most, for a comparable area on comparable ground.

  1. Broom finished concrete. The cheapest way to cover a large flat area, because it is placed in one operation.
  2. Standard concrete pavers. The residential default, and priced by the unit and the labor.
  3. Stamped concrete. A slab plus the stamping, coloring and sealing work.
  4. Large format or premium pavers. Fewer units, more expensive units, and less forgiving to lay.
  5. Flagstone. Every piece is different and every joint is fitted. This is stonework, and it is priced as a craft rather than as a surface.

The ranking flips if you extend the horizon. On the clay through much of north Georgia the ground moves, and when it moves a slab cracks permanently while a paver field can be lifted and reset in an afternoon. That difference is covered in the materials comparison.

Size works in your favor

Patios are one of the few jobs where the price per square foot falls as the job grows.

Getting a crew, a machine, a truck and a delivery of stone to your property costs the same whether the patio is a hundred square feet or four hundred. Spread over four times the area, that setup is a quarter of the burden.

The practical consequence: if you are torn between a patio that is just big enough and one that is comfortably big enough, the second one costs less per square foot than the first. That is not a sales argument, it is arithmetic, and it is the opposite of how most people assume it works.

The things that genuinely move the number

Access

This one outranks material choice more often than anything else. If a small machine can drive to the area, excavation and stone delivery are quick. If everything has to come through a gate by wheelbarrow, the same patio takes days longer.

Corner lots, open side yards and driveways that run to the back are cheap. Landlocked back gardens behind a house and a narrow gate are not.

Whether the ground has to be cut

A patio wants a level plane. If the ground already provides one, you are paying for a patio. If it does not, you are paying for a patio plus the excavation and a wall to hold the cut, and the wall is priced by its face area rather than by the patio.

This is the single biggest reason two houses on the same street get quotes that look unrelated.

Drainage

A new hard surface changes where rain goes. If the water can shed into planting or a low corner, nothing extra is needed. If it has to be collected and carried away, that is trenching, pipe and an outlet, all of it invisible in the finished photo. Layout and drainage are the same decision.

Shape and cuts

Every curve is a row of cut units and every cut is time on a saw. A rectangle with a soldier course border is the efficient shape. A curved patio with an inlaid pattern is a different level of labor for the same square footage.

Steps and level changes

Steps in hardscape are small structures. Each one has its own base, its own units and its own alignment, and a flight of three costs meaningfully more than the equivalent area of flat paving.

What is being built at the same time

Seat walls, a fire pit, a pergola footing, a porch slab or a deck stepping down onto the patio all add to the total and all cost less done together than done in three visits. The machine, the crew and the material delivery are already there.

Removing what is already there

Breaking out and hauling an old concrete slab is heavy, slow work and a full disposal load. It is often the largest single surprise on a patio quote and it belongs on the quote as its own line.

Repairs, resets and the long view

Two things happen to patios over time and both are cheaper than people fear.

Settling. A low spot in a paver field is lifted, the base corrected, and the units reset. The pavers themselves are reused. This is a normal maintenance event, not a failure.

Joints washing out. Sand migrates, especially where water runs across the patio. Sweeping in fresh joint material is straightforward, and polymeric sand slows the recurrence.

A slab offers neither of these. When a slab cracks or a corner settles, the options are living with it, a cosmetic overlay, or replacement. That is the real long-term argument between the two, and it is worth weighing against the up front difference.

Why there are no dollar figures here

Because a number here would be dishonest. Patio pricing is dominated by excavation, disposal and access, and those three vary more between two houses on the same street than the surface material varies across the whole product range.

A published average blends flat, open, easy sites with steep, tight ones and produces a figure that matches neither. We would rather look at the site and give you a number that means something. Ask us for one.

What a patio quote should itemize

  1. Total area, and the finished shape.
  2. Excavation depth and what happens to the excavated soil.
  3. Base thickness, the material used, and whether it is compacted in layers.
  4. Edge restraint, and what type.
  5. Joint material, and whether it is polymeric.
  6. Where the surface drains to.
  7. Steps, walls and any other vertical work, priced separately.

Ask for those seven from everyone quoting. The cheapest quote sometimes stays the cheapest after they are answered, and when it does, you can accept it knowing why.

Common questions

Questions people ask about this

Why is one patio quote so much cheaper than another?

Usually the depth of excavation and the amount of base material underneath. That work is invisible in the finished patio and it is most of the labor, so it is the first thing a thin quote leaves out.

Are pavers more expensive than concrete?

Per square foot, usually yes, because a paver patio is placed unit by unit and a slab is poured in one go. Across a longer life the gap narrows, because a settled paver can be lifted and reset and a cracked slab cannot.

Does a bigger patio cost more per square foot?

No, less. Getting a crew, a machine and material to your property costs the same whether the patio is small or large, so the setup spreads across more square feet on a bigger job.

What makes a patio quote go up the most?

Access, excavation and anything vertical. If a machine cannot reach the back of the house, or the ground has to be cut and held with a wall, those are the two lines that move the total.

How much does the edge matter?

A great deal. Without a proper edge restraint the outer courses creep outward and the joints open. It is a small line on a quote and the reason a patio still looks tight in year ten.
