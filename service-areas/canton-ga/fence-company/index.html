13,255 homes, and the ground under them

Canton put 13,255 homes on hillsides. The plats look like Woodstock and the ground doesn't. The subdivisions here were cut into slopes, not laid across flat land, so lots that look identical on a plat have a fence line running downhill on one side and along a contour on the other. The run length is the easy number. The fall along it is the one that decides how the job gets built.

2005 houses and an open choice

Half the houses here are newer than 2005, so most Canton fence jobs are first installations with nothing to match. That makes it worth actually weighing wood privacy against vinyl and aluminum, not defaulting to whatever the neighbor has. On sloped ground the choice narrows, because rigid preformed sections step and flexible built-on-site fences can follow.

What the bottom of the fence does on a hill

Every fence crossing a slope leaves a gap at the bottom or a rake at the top, and there are only three honest answers: rack the panels so the fence follows the ground, step them and fill the triangles, or grade the line first. Nobody enjoys the third one. What matters is choosing before the posts are set, because the post spacing is different for each and it can't be changed afterward.

My yard drops four feet along one side. Can a fence still look right?

Yes, and there are two ways to do it. Racking follows the ground and keeps a continuous line. Stepping keeps every section level and leaves gaps that get filled. Four feet over a side run is well inside what either can handle.
