14,380 homes and a settled ownership base

Nearly two thirds of Kennesaw's 14,380 homes are lived in by the people who own them. Fences in a town like that get maintained rather than inherited and abandoned, so what we find on arrival is usually a fence somebody has already been looking after, which changes the conversation from replacement to intervention.

Nineties houses, and fences at twenty-five

Most of these houses went up around 1998, which puts the original fence at about twenty-five, right at the end of a wood fence life without maintenance and about the middle of one with it. What survives that long is wood that was stained, and what does not is wood that was not. Where the posts are still sound and the panels have gone, repair rather than replacement is a legitimate answer and it is worth asking about before the whole thing comes out.

The bit that fails first

On a fence of this age the failure sequence is predictable. Pickets go before rails, rails go before posts, and posts go at ground level rather than in the middle. So the useful diagnostic is not how the fence looks from the house but whether a post moves when you push it. If the posts hold, most of what is wrong is surface work. If they do not, the surface work is wasted money.

How do I know if my posts are still good?

Push each one hard at shoulder height. Movement at the base means the post has gone at ground level, which is where they always go. If none of them move, the fence is worth saving.
