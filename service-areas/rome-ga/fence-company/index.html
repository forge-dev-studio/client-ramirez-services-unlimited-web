16,566 homes across one city boundary

Rome holds 16,566 homes and didn't build them in one go. Close-set older streets have lots that are narrow and deep, mid-century blocks have generous side yards, and the newer edges open up. Three different fence jobs, one city name. The estimate depends far more on which of those you're in than on the material you pick.

Nothing new about the fence that's already there

Half of Rome was up before 1974, so most fence work here is replacement, not first installation, and what's coming down is usually chain link that has been up longer than the current owner has been there. Chain link of that age is often still structurally fine at the terminal posts even when the fabric has gone, which is worth checking before committing to a full tear-out. Where the fabric is the only problem, repair is a genuinely cheaper answer than replacement.

Long boundaries and how they get broken up

On a deep Rome lot the side runs do most of the work and the back run is short. That changes where the gates go: two walk gates on the sides beats one at the back for anybody who actually uses the yard. Corner count is the other thing that moves a number, because every corner post is a braced post and a braced post is a different job from a line post.
