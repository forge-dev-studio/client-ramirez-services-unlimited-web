A deck is a frame with a floor on it

Footings, posts, beams and joists carry every pound of a deck.

Nobody looks at any of them.

What people look at is the surface. Which is why a tired deck so often gets new boards and nothing else.

The boards go on, the deck looks new, and the frame under them keeps doing exactly what it was already doing.

We build the structure to carry more than the deck will ever hold.

That costs very little at the start of a job. It is the most expensive thing on a deck to put right afterward.

The ledger, which is where decks fail

A ledger is one board, bolted through the siding into the framing of the house.

On an attached deck it carries roughly half the load.

Water is what beats it. Rain runs down the siding, reaches the top edge of the ledger and has to go somewhere.

Flashing sends it back out.

No flashing and it goes behind the board instead, sits against the fasteners, and works on the house band year after year.

That failure is quiet. It shows up as a deck that feels slightly wrong near the door, long before anything visible has moved.

Footings hold the deck. Dirt does not

A post set straight into soil is standing in the wettest place on the property.

It will hold for a while. It will not hold forever.

Footings go down past the loose material to ground that stays put through a wet spring and a dry August.

Posts land on top of them on anchors, above the soil line, where air can reach the end grain.

How many footings a deck needs is arithmetic rather than preference. Beam span sets post spacing, and post spacing sets the hole count.

Joists, spacing and the bounce test

Joist spacing is decided by what is going on top.

Solid wood tolerates a wider spacing than most composite boards do. So choosing the surface is part of framing the deck, not a decision that comes later.

Blocking is the piece people leave out.

Short lengths of joist material set between the joists stop them rolling under load.

That is the difference between a deck that feels solid underfoot and one that moves when somebody walks past your chair.

Fasteners get chosen for the wood they are going into. Treated lumber eats the wrong hardware.

What actually happens

The order a deck gets built in

  1. Layout, before anything is dug

    We mark the footprint on the ground and stand in it with you. Height off the ground, where the stairs land and which way the boards run are all easier to change now than later.

  2. Footings

    Holes go below the loose topsoil onto ground that will not move. Count and diameter follow the beam spans above them, so a wider deck means more footings rather than heavier guesswork.

  3. Ledger and posts

    The ledger gets fastened into the house band, flashed so water runs out rather than in. Posts land on the footings and get anchored, never buried in dirt.

  4. Beams, joists and blocking

    Joists are hung at a spacing chosen for the decking that is going on top of them. Blocking between joists stops the frame twisting and takes the bounce out of a long span.

  5. Decking and the walk through

    Boards go down and the offcuts, banding and old material leave with us. You get the yard back the day the work finishes.

Questions we get asked

Before you call

What decides how large a deck can be?

Span does. Every joist can only reach so far before it needs a beam under it, and every beam can only reach so far before it needs another post. A bigger deck is not more decking, it is more structure, and that is where the work goes.

Attached to the house, or freestanding?

Attached is normal and it is what most back doors want. Freestanding avoids putting any new fastening into the house band, which is worth doing when the wall behind the deck is brick veneer or when the band is already soft. We check the wall before deciding.

How is the deck actually held to the house?

Through the ledger, which is a board bolted into the framing of the house behind the siding. It carries half the weight of the deck. Done right it is flashed so rain sheds away from the fasteners. Done wrong it is the first thing to rot.

My back yard slopes hard. Does that change anything?

It changes post height and it changes how the stairs land, so it changes the price of the frame more than the price of the surface. Tell us roughly how far the ground drops when you call, and we can say what the frame is likely to need.
