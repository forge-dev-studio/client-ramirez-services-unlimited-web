Deck Staining and Sealing

A finish fails for two reasons: it went onto a surface that wasn't ready, or it went on in the wrong weather.

Refinishing is mostly preparation

The coat itself is the short part of the job. Everything before it is the long part, and it's the part that decides the result. A deck left alone for a few summers isn't one surface.

It's bare wood in the traffic lanes, old finish under the furniture, mildew on the shaded end, and mill glaze on any board replaced last year. Put one coat across all of that and you get four different colors and four different lifespans.

Weather is a specification, not a preference

Every finish sold carries a temperature range and a humidity range on the label. Both are frequently outside what a Georgia afternoon offers. Too hot and the carrier evaporates at the surface before it can take pigment down into the wood. Too damp and it never fully cures. Rain inside the drying window and the whole coat lifts.

So the job is scheduled against a forecast instead of a calendar, and it moves when the forecast moves. That's inconvenient once. Redoing a deck is inconvenient for a lot longer.

Boards aren't one moisture content

Wood doesn't dry uniformly. The end of a deck that catches morning sun is ready before the end that sits under a tree, and the underside of a board never matches the top. So the drying step gets measured, not eyeballed.

A surface can look dry, feel dry, and still hold enough water to reject a penetrating finish.

What a coat can and can't fix

Finish stops the sun bleaching the wood and slows how fast water moves in and out. It does that well.

It doesn't glue a split board back together. It doesn't stop a cupped board cupping, and it does nothing at all for a soft joist under your feet. If any of that is going on, the honest order is structure first and color afterward.

How often this comes back around

Horizontal boards in full sun give up their finish faster than vertical rails in shade. So a deck doesn't wear out evenly, and it rarely needs everything doing at once.

The traffic lane in front of the door is usually the first place to go. Catching that early is a wash and a coat. Leaving it is a strip and a coat.

What actually happens

The sequence, and why the order is fixed

  1. Strip what's already on there

    An old film coat has to come off before a new one goes on. Skipping this is the single most common reason a refinished deck peels in sheets the following summer.

  2. Wash, then neutralize

    Cleaners used on decks are alkaline and they leave the wood chemically open. Rinsing alone doesn't close it. A brightener resets the surface so the finish soaks in evenly instead of blotching.

  3. Let it dry, and measure, not guess

    Wet wood won't take a penetrating finish. Depending on the week that's two days or it's five, and the shaded end of a deck dries slower than the end in full sun.

  4. Repair before finish, never after

    Loose boards, popped fasteners and split ends get dealt with while the deck is bare. A fresh board dropped into a finished deck never matches, and it always shows.

  5. Coat, watching the surface temperature

    Boards in direct afternoon sun can be far hotter than the air. Finish flashes off a hot board before it can soak in, so the work follows the shade around the deck, not the clock.

Questions we get asked

Before you call

When is the best time of year to do this in Georgia?

Late spring after the pollen has washed off, or early fall once the worst of the heat has broken. Midsummer is workable early in the day. The wet stretch in the middle of winter isn't, because the wood never dries enough to take anything.

How long does the deck stay off limits?

Foot traffic usually the next day, furniture and grills longer. You get the actual window at scheduling, because it moves with humidity, and it's worth planning around instead of discovering the morning after.

My last stain peeled. Why?

Almost always one of two things. It went over a previous coat that had not been stripped, so the new film had nothing to hold but the old film. Or it went onto wood that was still damp, so it sat on top instead of soaking in.

Can a brand new deck be stained right away?

Not usually. Fresh pressure-treated lumber leaves the yard wet and needs to give that moisture up first. Putting a finish on it too early traps water in the board.

Do you finish railings and stairs as well as the floor?

Yes, and they take longer per square foot than the deck surface does, because everything is edges. It's worth being clear at the quote about whether rails are included.
