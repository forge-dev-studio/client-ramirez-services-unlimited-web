Fence Repair and Gates

Most fences do not fail all at once. They fail in one or two places, and the question is whether the rest is worth saving.

The five minute survey you can do yourself

Walk the fence and push on every post.

That is it. That is the diagnosis.

Write down how many move and where they are.

Bunched at one end usually means ground conditions. Spread evenly along the run usually means age.

Then look at what the moving posts have in common.

Under a downspout, at the bottom of a slope, beside a flower bed that gets watered.

Those posts have had a wetter life than the rest, and they went first for a reason that will apply again.

Two ways a post fails, and they cost different amounts

Grab a post that moves and watch where the movement happens.

If it hinges at ground level while the concrete stays put, the wood has gone.

That post is finished and a new one goes in.

If the post and its footing tilt together as one lump, the wood may be fine.

Clay that has been through enough wet and dry cycles simply stops gripping.

That is often a reset rather than a replacement, and it is a much shorter job.

The test is a screwdriver at the soil line. Sound wood resists. Punky wood takes the blade to the handle.

Why gates drop

A gate is a rectangle held on one edge.

Gravity has been trying to turn it into a parallelogram since the day it went on.

What resists that is a diagonal. Either a brace from the bottom hinge corner up to the top latch corner, or a cable tensioned the other way.

Without one, the free corner sinks and the gate starts to catch.

The two diagnoses are easy to tell apart.

Measure the gate corner to corner in both directions.

If the diagonals match, the gate is still square and the post it hangs on has leaned.

If they do not match, the gate has racked and needs bracing rather than resetting.

The third cause is simpler and more common than either.

Hinge screws driven into end grain, or into a hollow post with nothing behind it, gradually pull out.

The gate then sags at the hinge itself while everything around it is fine.

Repairing a run costs more per post than building one

This surprises people, so it is worth saying plainly.

On a new fence the posts go in as one operation.

Every hole is dug in sequence, the concrete is mixed once, and the run moves at a steady pace.

A repair means pulling a post that is trapped between two panels, then breaking out an old footing that does not want to come.

The new post has to meet a line that already exists and cannot be adjusted. Each one is a small, awkward job on its own.

Which is why the honest advice flips at some point.

A few bad posts are worth chasing. A run where most of them are going is cheaper to replace than to repair twice.

What actually happens

Working out what is worth saving

  1. Push every post

    Hand on the post, weight against it. A post that moves has failed. Counting how many out of the run have failed is the fastest honest answer to repair or replace.

  2. Separate two different failures

    A post that pivots at ground level has lost its wood. A post that moves as a unit with its footing has lost its hold on the soil. The first needs a new post, the second can sometimes be reset.

  3. Check the run for square

    Sight down the fence from one end. A wave means the posts are still doing their job and the rails have sagged. A lean that grows toward one end means the tension in the run is pulling.

  4. Gates get measured, not eyeballed

    Corner to corner across the gate frame, both ways. If the two measurements differ, the gate has racked and no amount of hinge adjustment puts it back.

  5. A count, then a choice

    You get the number of bad posts, the state of the rails and boards, and what each option costs you in time on site. The choice stays yours.

Questions we get asked

Before you call

At what point should I just replace the whole fence?

There is no fixed line, but a useful test is how many posts have gone. One or two in a long run is a repair. When a third of them move, replacing costs less than chasing them one at a time, because each individual reset carries the same setup as a whole run.

Why does my gate drag on the ground?

Two possibilities. The hinge post has leaned, which drops the whole gate, or the gate frame itself has racked out of square. Measure the diagonals of the gate. Equal diagonals mean the post moved. Unequal diagonals mean the gate did.

Can a leaning post be reset without replacing it?

Sometimes. If the wood at ground level is still sound and the footing simply lost its grip, resetting works. If a screwdriver goes into the post at grade with no effort, resetting buys a season at most.

A tree took out a section in a storm. Do you do just that part?

Yes. Storm damage is often the easiest repair on this page because the failure is obvious and local. The worthwhile question is whether the posts either side of the gap were already weak and the tree simply found them.

Can you match my existing fence?

Usually close, rarely exact. Wood that has weathered for a few summers has a color that new lumber will not have for a couple of years. Vinyl and coated metal from a discontinued line can be harder still. You get an honest description of what the repair will look like before it happens.
