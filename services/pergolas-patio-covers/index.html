The kit is the competition, and it is worth saying so

Most people searching for a pergola are looking at boxed ones.

There are a lot of them and they are cheaper.

For a small square patio in the middle of a lawn, one of them may well be the right call.

What a kit cannot do is adapt.

It arrives at a fixed footprint with fixed post positions.

Your patio has a step in it, a door that swings out, a downspout in one corner and a grill that has to live somewhere.

Building in place means the structure is laid out around all of that.

Posts land where they do not block a route. The footprint follows the slab edge instead of floating inside it.

Beam sizes then follow the spans that result, rather than the spans dictating a shape.

What it lands on decides most of the job

A shade structure only ever starts from one of three surfaces, and they are not variations of each other.

An existing concrete slab takes anchored post bases. That is quick and clean, provided the slab is thick enough and sound where each post lands.

A slab already cracked through at that point is not a place to anchor.

An existing deck is the fussiest.

Load goes through the decking into the frame below, so a beam, a post or a new footing has to sit directly under every pergola post.

That work happens underneath, before anything visible starts.

Open ground is the simplest structurally. Footings go exactly where the design wants them and nothing has to be worked around.

Wind lifts what it cannot get through

A solid patio cover is pushed down and sideways. That is straightforward, and it is what most people picture.

An open pergola is different.

Air moving across the top of a set of rafters generates lift, and the whole structure tries to rise.

Which is why boxed structures on spike anchors work loose over a couple of storm seasons with nothing visible having failed.

The answer is at the base of the post, not the top of it.

Anchors have to resist being pulled upward. On a deck that means tying the post to framing that reaches the ground, not to the deck surface.

Span, and why post count is not a preference

People start with the number of posts they want and work backward. It works better the other way around.

A beam of a given depth can cross a given distance.

Ask it to cross further and it deflects, and a beam that visibly sags makes a structure look homemade for the rest of its life.

Wanting a wide clear opening is completely reasonable.

It is paid for in beam depth rather than in wishful thinking, and that is worth knowing at the design stage rather than at the disappointment stage.

What actually happens

What has to be settled before anything goes up

  1. What it is standing on

    A slab, a deck or open ground are three different anchoring problems. We look at what is already there first, because that decides the post detail and it cannot be changed later.

  2. Post count follows the span

    The distance a beam can cross without help sets how many posts you need. Wanting four posts instead of six is a beam question, and it is answered with a bigger beam rather than with optimism.

  3. Held down as well as up

    Wind pushes a roof down and lifts an open structure. The connection at the base of each post has to resist being pulled upward, which is a different fastening from one that only has to stop the post sliding.

  4. Attached, and the wall it attaches to

    A ledger against the house means opening a line in the wall cladding and closing it back up so water sheds outward. On brick veneer or on a wall that is already wet, freestanding is often the better answer.

  5. Rafter spacing chosen for a time of day

    Open rafters throw stripes that move through the afternoon. Tighter spacing means deeper shade and more material. That is a decision, and it is worth making it standing in the space at the hour you will use it.

Questions we get asked

Before you call

Why build one on site instead of buying a kit?

Because a kit is one size and your patio is not. Built in place, the footprint matches the slab, the post positions miss the door swing and the walkway, and the beam is sized for the span you actually have rather than the span the box assumed.

Can a pergola go on my existing deck?

Often, but the deck has to be able to take it. Pergola posts concentrate load and uplift into four or six small points, and a deck framed for people standing on it may need blocking or a new footing under each post. That gets checked before quoting, not after.

Attached to the house or freestanding?

Freestanding puts no new fastening into your wall at all, which removes the main long-term risk. Attached uses two fewer posts and sits tighter to the back door. We look at the wall and the roofline and tell you which one makes sense on your house.

What is the difference between a pergola and a patio cover?

A pergola has an open top made of rafters. A patio cover has a solid roof over it. They are framed similarly and they are used completely differently, and the roof version has to shed water somewhere you have thought about.

Cedar, treated pine or aluminum?

Cedar looks the part and stays straighter. Treated pine costs less and can be stained to match an existing deck. Aluminum never needs refinishing and reads as more modern. All three work outdoors here.
